Issue 02 / Fall & Winter · Prime Riding Season

South Florida Prime Riding Season: What to Inspect Before the First Big Run.

November through April is what the rest of the country dreams about. The 3pm downpours have cleared, the humidity breaks, mornings drop into the 60s, and riders are pulling bikes out of carports from West Palm down to Miami. US-27 to Lake Okeechobee, Alligator Alley, Card Sound Road into the Keys — this is peak season.

The catch: a bike that sat under a cover through four months of monsoon season is not ready for an eight-hour run just because you wiped the dust off the seat. Between humidity, salt air, and ethanol fuel breakdown, idle bikes hide problems that only show up at 75 mph.

A bike that sat all summer isn't ready for the highway just because it starts in the driveway.

The monsoon fuel-pump trap

The most common breakdown we diagnose when the dry season starts is a failing or seized fuel pump — hard starts, backfiring, or stalling 10 to 15 minutes in. Clogged gas-cap weep holes let rainwater pool and drop into the tank. South Florida E10 pump gas is hygroscopic: water bonds with the ethanol and sinks to the bottom, right where the pump pickup sits. Instead of fuel, the pump draws a corrosive water-and-ethanol slurry.

The tell: a loud screeching whine on prime instead of a clean two-second hum; idles on the stand but starves under load; cuts out after 15 minutes once the pump heats up. The fix is to drain the tank, scope the interior for flash rust, flush it, and replace the pump and filter before you cook your injectors.

The pre-run checklist

Four systems before you load the saddlebags. Tires: our flat, straight roads square off the center strip and leave a ridge that makes the bike resist leaning, then fall into the turn — and replace any tire with a DOT date over five years no matter the tread. Brake fluid: DOT 4 and 5.1 absorb water through the lines; dark, tea-colored fluid boils under load and fades the lever to the grip. Battery: summer heat kills the plates and the first cold snap exposes it — you want 12.6 to 12.8V at rest, and it's done if it drops below 10.5V cranking.

By platform

Harley & V-twins: milky primary oil means condensation — flush it; listen for compensator slap on cold start; inspect the drive belt for stone punctures and the stator plug near the lower crankcase for grime. Japanese sport bikes: salt air pits fork stanchions that then slice the seals onto your brakes; a chain left un-lubed after soaked rides rusts and robs power; clean caked radiator fins before you sit in A1A beach traffic. Italian & European: check hydraulic clutch slave cylinders for weeping; these ECUs hate low voltage and throw ABS and quickshifter faults below spec; and Ducati timing belts age out regardless of miles — a snapped belt is valve-to-piston contact.

Riding season only lasts so long. Spending the best weekends waiting for a tow is avoidable with 45 minutes of bench prep. If your bike sat unstarted or took on summer rain, get it on a lift before the long miles.

Issue 01 / Summer Service

Summer Is Here. Prep the Bike for Sunset Rides.

You walk toward the bike while the heat is still lifting off the pavement. Softer light, cleaner shadows, a road that stops looking like a commute and starts looking like an invitation. This is the hour motorcycles make the most sense. You are not escaping the day. You are finishing it right.

The sunset ride looks effortless from the outside, but the bike does not feel it as a postcard. It feels the hot start, the traffic crawl, the sudden throttle, the brake squeeze at the last yellow, the fan cycling, the chain load, and every small weakness you hoped would stay quiet.

You do not prep the bike because you fear the ride. You prep it because you respect it.

Start with the tires: pressure cold, tread read like evidence, sidewalls checked. A cupping front feels vague in a lean; old rubber does not get safer at sunset, just prettier. Then heat: coolant level, fan operation, radiator clear. A bike that suddenly runs hotter than usual is talking — listen before it shouts. Brakes get the same respect: squeeze the lever and notice firm, soft, spongy, pulsing, or late.

The fastest way to catch a problem is to notice the first difference, not the final failure. Did it sit too long? Did the battery struggle? Did the idle change? When you feel that first difference, send it. That is the whole point of having a shop you trust on the other end of a message.
